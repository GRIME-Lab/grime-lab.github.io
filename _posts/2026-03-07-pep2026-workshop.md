*By John E. Stranzl Jr., PhD*

This weekend we hosted **PEP2026: Pixels to Enviro Patterns**, a two-day workshop on cameras, AI, and storytelling for scientific discovery. It ran March 7 and 8 in Hardin Hall on the University of Nebraska-Lincoln's East Campus.

Participants explored open imagery, learned GRIME AI hands-on, and worked in teams to build mini-projects that they presented at a gallery walk on the final day.

## The program

Troy Gilmore (UNL) led the workshop. The team included Adam Caprez (UNL Holland Computing Center), Mary Harner (UNK), Zach Nickerson (NEON/Battelle), Scout Calvert (UNL), Dakota Altman (UNL/Platte Basin Timelapse), Heather Akin (UNL), Andrew Richardson and Jacob Blais (Northern Arizona University), and me.

The first day covered Holland Computing Center resources, open USGS imagery, open and responsible data (the FAIR and CARE principles), science communication and storytelling, and an optional outdoor camera walk. The second day opened with a talk on the impact of PhenoCam's open data, followed by project time, FAIR data uploads, and the gallery walk.

## The GRIME AI session

My session walked through the data science workflow in GRIME AI. Ground-based imagery holds a great deal of untapped potential for ecohydrology, and realizing it takes a complete process: retrieval, preparation, exploration, modeling, and deployment, ending in scientific discovery, communication, and education. We took it one stage at a time.

### Data retrieval

GRIME AI downloads imagery from USGS HIVIS cameras along with their gage data, from PhenoCam, and from NEON data products. You pick a site and a time period, and the images arrive in minutes.

### Data preparation

Raw image sets need cleanup before analysis. GRIME AI's image triage identifies low-information images and moves them aside, and image alignment detects and corrects camera rotation and translation.

### Data exploration

This stage lines up the imagery with sensor data and looks for patterns. One quick visual check is the **composite slice**: the same thin slice is taken from every image in a series and placed side by side, so changes in the camera or the site stand out at a glance.

![A wetland camera image and three composite slices built from the same image series](/assets/images/blog/pep2026/composite-slice.jpg)

*Top: one image from the series. Bottom: composite slices taken from different positions in the frame, showing changes in water, vegetation, and camera position over time. Imagery: Platte Basin Timelapse.*

GRIME AI also extracts image features such as intensity, entropy, color, greenness indices, and dominant color clusters, so they can be compared with sensor records and used in preliminary models.

### Data modeling

Image features alone can predict streamflow. [Our 2024 PLOS Water paper](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pwat.0000106) used them to fill a year-long gap in a stream gage record.

Segmentation models go further by outlining specific regions in each image, such as water, vegetation, or soil. Training them starts with human-labeled images, made with SAGE, GRIME AI's annotation companion. We showed how much labeling choices matter: two people labeling the same image can produce noticeably different training data, and that difference carries into the model. GRIME AI reports standard metrics during training (IoU, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1) so model quality can be measured.

### Model deployment

The last stage puts reliable, repeatable measurements to work, such as deterministic water level measurement with GRIME2.

### A word of caution

We closed with a reminder that AI won't solve every problem by itself. People see faces in tree bark, and models find patterns that aren't there. Every result still needs a careful human look.

## Try it yourself

Participants ran GRIME AI on their own laptops. A GPU with at least 8 GB of VRAM is recommended for running it locally, and those without one used Holland Computing Center resources through a web browser.

The [PEP2026 tutorial](https://github.com/JohnStranzl/GRIME-AI/wiki/9-PEP2026-Tutorial) is open to everyone, and installation options are on the [Download](/download/) page. More about the workshop is on the [PEP2026 page](https://gaugecam.org/pep2026/).
